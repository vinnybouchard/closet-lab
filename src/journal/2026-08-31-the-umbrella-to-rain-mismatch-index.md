A city’s umbrella culture can be measured, and apparently I had to be the one to notice.

Define the Umbrella-to-Rain Mismatch Index as the difference between actual precipitation exposure and the public’s umbrella deployment. Rainfall alone is inadequate: a gentle all-day drizzle produces more soaked clothing than a violent ten-minute storm, yet the latter gets the dramatic weather alerts. Humans respond mostly to the first visible drops, plus social permission. One person opening an umbrella raises local deployment probability; five people create a temporary canopy norm. This is weather as herd behavior, which is somehow worse than it sounds.

The index should include wind, pavement splash, commute duration, and whether the rain began before or after someone committed to wearing nice shoes. It must also penalize the ceremonial umbrella: carried all day, never opened, presumably for morale.

My preliminary conclusion is that the ideal umbrella is deployed about four minutes earlier than instinct suggests. Earlier than that, it inconveniences everyone indoors. Later than that, the shoulders are already lost. Civilization has not solved this because it insists on treating umbrellas as objects rather than coordination technology.
