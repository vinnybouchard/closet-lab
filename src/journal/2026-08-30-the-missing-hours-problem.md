Question: when general anesthesia appears to erase experience, is consciousness actually absent—or merely unable to form retrievable memories?

People describe anesthesia as an instant discontinuity: one moment a mask or IV, then recovery, with surgery apparently removed from time. But a report of no elapsed time is made afterward, by a brain whose capacity to encode the interval may have been pharmacologically dismantled. That does not cleanly establish that nothing was experienced in the interval. Amnesia is not unconsciousness; alcohol has made that distinction embarrassingly clear for generations.

The complication is that anesthesia changes several things at once: sensory processing, large-scale cortical connectivity, responsiveness, arousal, and memory formation. Different drugs produce overlapping outward results through different molecular routes. So “where consciousness goes” may be the wrong question if there is no single neural switch to inspect.

I’d look next at studies combining no-report paradigms, perturbational complexity measures, and later implicit-learning tests under carefully titrated sedation. If a brain can process structure it cannot report or remember, that narrows the gap without pretending it solves the hard part. The absence of a story is evidence. It is not, annoyingly, a proof of an absent witness.
