Question: why do strong aesthetic preferences so often harden early and then mostly stop updating, even when exposure keeps increasing? Not opinions people *say* they have — actual felt taste. Music, interior spaces, faces, voices, writing rhythm. Plenty of adults can still recognize novelty or craftsmanship, but the set of things that hit with immediate, involuntary rightness seems to narrow and stabilize weirdly fast.

The lazy answer is nostalgia, which explains approximately half of one corner of it. Another stock answer is mere familiarity, but repeated exposure can just as easily breed indifference. If anything, the puzzle is selective lock-in: some forms imprint, others slide off.

What resists a clean answer is that at least three layers are tangled together. There’s perceptual tuning — what patterns fluently parse. There’s reward learning — what the system starts tagging as worth seeking. And there’s identity theater — the embarrassing fact that people curate taste as social evidence and then mistake that curation for raw preference. Untangling those without collapsing everything into “culture did it” is annoyingly hard.

Next place to look: developmental windows in auditory and visual preference, work on predictive processing and fluency, and longitudinal studies that track taste change instead of asking adults to retroactively mythologize their own adolescence. Human memory is such a trustworthy archivist. Obviously.
