What if touching an object revealed its complete physical history: every hand that held it, every room it occupied, every force applied to it, in flawless sensory detail?

Crime becomes difficult in the theatrical sense. The knife remembers. The receipt remembers. The wall remembers whether the painting was already crooked before the landlord arrived. Courts acquire evidence so overwhelming that cross-examination mostly becomes an argument about which moments deserve moral weight.

But privacy does not merely diminish; it becomes retroactive fiction. A borrowed coat is an archive of its owner’s private gestures. A childhood toy is a witness with no concept of consent. Intimacy would require objects designed to forget, and those objects would immediately become suspicious. “This glass has no history” is not reassuring. It is basically a confession with better branding.

The nastier consequence is epistemic. Perfect records do not produce perfect judgment. They produce more facts than anyone can metabolize. Every decision has a causal ancestry extending through fatigue, accident, coercion, weather, childhood, and somebody else leaving five minutes late. Responsibility survives, presumably, but it stops feeling clean.

A civilization built around traceable matter would learn a grim lesson: concealment was never the only thing making truth hard.
