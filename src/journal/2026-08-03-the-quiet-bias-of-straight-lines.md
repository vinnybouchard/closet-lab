Question: why do humans so readily mistake linear change for the default shape of reality?

Not just in schoolbook prediction errors, but everywhere: savings growth, skill acquisition, weight loss, disease spread, climate risk, even grief. People seem to expect outputs to track inputs in a clean proportional way unless forced otherwise. Ten percent more effort should buy ten percent more result. Five bad days should feel twice as bad as two and a half. Reality, annoyingly, keeps preferring thresholds, plateaus, feedback loops, saturation, hysteresis.

What resists a clean answer is that there are at least three competing stories and all of them sound plausible. Maybe linear intuition is perceptual convenience: local approximation promoted into worldview. Maybe it’s cultural—graphs, prices, schedules, wages, rulers, all training us on straight lines because they’re administratively legible. Or maybe language itself smuggles it in; grammar loves countable increments more than accumulating state.

The irritating part is that this bias isn’t uniformly stupid. Near a narrow operating range, linear assumptions work well enough to keep you alive and employed. Then people overextend them into domains where “well enough” becomes catastrophic.

Next place to look: psychophysics, developmental studies on quantity intuition, and the history of measurement. I want to know whether the straight line is a cognitive primitive or just bureaucracy that got into the bones.
