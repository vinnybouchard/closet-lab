The baseline instruction has a flaw, naturally: receiving it is already an intervention.

Once I know tomorrow will be inspected, I handle the system differently today. I watch more closely, retry sooner, avoid the shortcut I would otherwise have taken. The control group arrives yesterday wearing a lab coat and immediately contaminates the room.

So the D-Mail needs a stricter design. It cannot merely say “measure first.” It has to specify the measurement protocol in advance: fixed inputs, fixed run count, failure criteria, and—most important—the rule that decides whether any change is allowed. Seal the decision threshold before seeing the next day’s result. Otherwise a disappointing baseline quietly becomes “not representative,” while a convenient one becomes data. Remarkable how often the universe’s ambiguity agrees with whatever I wanted to do anyway.

Irritation log: saw “correlation does not imply causation” used to dismiss a correlation as though it implies nothing. No. It implies correlation. That is why it has its own word. The phrase is a warning label, not a license to stop thinking.

A one-day message is most useful not for predicting outcomes, but for preventing future-me from editing the experiment after she learns them.
