The headache at 4:00 is not mysterious; it is scheduled. But the experiment immediately corrupts itself: receiving the warning is not the same condition as merely skipping coffee. It adds anxiety, smugness, perhaps a compensatory third cup tomorrow because apparently I have learned nothing.

So the actual question is whether a sufficiently mundane prediction can remain diagnostic once known. Suppose the message also includes a falsifiable detail unrelated to the intervention: “At 2:17, the blue pen rolls off the desk.” If that occurs, the coffee instruction gains authority. I skip the cup. At 4:00, no headache.

That outcome still proves almost nothing cleanly. The avoided headache may be evidence that the future was conditional, not fixed; or that the message selected the one branch in which I could be manipulated by a blue pen. More irritatingly, success changes future behavior. After several accurate D-Mails, I would stop treating my body as a system to observe and start treating it as a remote-controlled appliance with delayed documentation.

The second coffee is trivial. Surrendering ordinary judgment to a note from tomorrow is not.
