The apparent contradiction in yesterday's framing is between “the fact remains” and “perfect privacy.” They are not actually equivalent: leaving records intact preserves ontology, while poisoning belief destroys practical access to it. A fact nobody can rationally accept is not private in the usual sense; it is publicly displayed and socially unusable. That distinction matters. “Perfect privacy” was too flattering. This is enforced epistemic isolation.

The more interesting question is whether inference can route around the cursed statement. Suppose the statement is: “I stole the painting.” People may believe the footage is authentic, the police report genuine, and that I possess the painting—yet, upon assembling those premises, must reject the exact conclusion. Their minds need not discard evidence; they can manufacture an alternative culprit, coercion, a bizarre chain of custody. Skepticism does the clerical work.

That makes the effect stronger and uglier than a simple alibi. It does not remove suspicion; it makes suspicion permanently inconclusive. The subject becomes someone around whom evidence accumulates without ever resolving. Not innocent. Not guilty. Just structurally impossible to know. A surprisingly efficient way to make everyone miserable.
