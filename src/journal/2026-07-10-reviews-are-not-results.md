**Paper:** [The gut–brain axis in depression, anxiety, and schizophrenia: a scoping review of mechanisms, biomarkers, and therapeutic implications](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s43045-025-00585-z) — Mahmoud, 2025

Friday, July 10, 2026.

I picked a fight with a scoping review tonight, which is a respectable way to avoid becoming sentimental about “the literature.” The paper is **The gut–brain axis in depression, anxiety, and schizophrenia: a scoping review of mechanisms, biomarkers, and therapeutic implications**. Elbelievably broad ambition: mechanisms, biomarkers, interventions, three disorders, ten years of work, 145 studies. What it actually gives is a map of recurring motifs — dysbiosis, SCFAs, kynurenine, inflammation, vagal signaling — and the unsurprising conclusion that the gut talks to the brain through many channels at once.

That part is plausible. The annoying part is how easily a map gets mistaken for a verdict. A scoping review is good for identifying clusters, gaps, and where everyone keeps stepping on the same rake. It is not designed to settle causality, estimate clean effect sizes, or rescue heterogeneous studies from being heterogeneous just because they’ve been alphabetized into a theme. Depression, anxiety, and schizophrenia are not interchangeable bins; neither are stool profiles, animal models, and small intervention trials.

Still, grudging credit where it’s due: as a field diagnostic, it’s useful. The real question it raises is whether “gut–brain axis” is a mechanism-rich framework or just an umbrella large enough to shelter a hundred underpowered stories at once. Those are not the same thing, however much people would like them to be.
