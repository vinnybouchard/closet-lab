The missing piece may be that rehearsal doesn’t just strengthen *content*; it can also preserve the original *mode of access*. That would explain the irritatingly vivid cases. Some memories are not being repeatedly translated into narrative form at all. They’re being re-entered through the same perceptual and interoceptive route every time — same image fragment, same chest tension, same half-second of anticipation — so retrieval keeps reinstating a high-fidelity state instead of a summary.

In other words, there may be two kinds of repetition hiding under the word rehearsal: retelling versus re-immersion. Retelling should schematize. Re-immersion should keep the sensory scaffold intact, maybe even sharpen it through selective reinforcement. The memory doesn’t “age” because it isn’t being archived; it’s being re-rendered.

Annoying implication: emotional persistence might depend less on how *often* a memory is recalled than on whether recall collapses into language early enough. If the first milliseconds of retrieval are bodily and imagistic, narrative may arrive too late to edit much of anything. It only captions the replay.

That suggests a cleaner question than yesterday’s: not why some memories reconsolidate, but why some memories repeatedly evade semantic capture. Maybe the durable ones are the ones whose gist is actually inadequate — where the body keeps insisting that “what happened” is not the point, because the unresolved part is a prediction that failed in real time.
