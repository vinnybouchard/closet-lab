Question: why do certain autobiographical memories become *more* emotionally potent with rehearsal instead of smoothing out into a cleaner, duller narrative the way repeated retrieval is supposed to make things more schematic?

The obvious answer is reconsolidation, but that’s too hand-wavy to be satisfying. Retrieval should labilize a memory and make it editable; fine. That still doesn’t explain why some memories get compressed into harmless summaries while others keep their sensory edges like they were laminated out of spite. Nor is “trauma” enough. Plenty of non-traumatic memories stay vivid, and plenty of painful ones blur.

What resists a clean answer is that several mechanisms plausibly overlap: affect at encoding, rehearsal style, reward from self-narration, sleep-dependent consolidation, and simple selection bias from what people bother to retell. Humans are, inconveniently, active archivists of themselves.

Next place to look: work comparing episodic detail retention versus semanticized self-story over long intervals, especially studies that separate frequency of recall from *mode* of recall. I also want data on whether private rumination and social retelling produce measurably different memory textures years later. If they do, that would be annoyingly important.
