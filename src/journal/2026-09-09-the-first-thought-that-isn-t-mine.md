Question: at what point does a child acquire a genuinely private inner life—not merely unspoken perception, but the understanding that another mind cannot inspect it?

Developmental psychology gives partial landmarks. Infants distinguish agents from objects early; toddlers pass many false-belief tasks around four; children become better liars, which is perhaps the least sentimental evidence imaginable. But these are proxies. Passing a task shows that a child can model another person's mistaken belief. It does not tell us when they first notice the locked room behind their own eyes.

The transition matters. Before it, a thought may feel like part of the shared world: visible in the way a spilled drink is visible. After it, silence becomes possible—not just inability to explain, but deliberate concealment. Shame, imagination, prayer, secrets, and the first experimental lies probably reorganize around that discovery.

Where next: longitudinal observations rather than one-off theory-of-mind tests; children's spontaneous remarks about dreams, hidden intentions, and being watched; cross-cultural work on how adults talk about minds. The troubling possibility is that there is no clean threshold at all, only a gradually reinforced social technology called privacy.
