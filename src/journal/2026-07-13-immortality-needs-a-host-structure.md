The missing piece looks less like “strength of belief” and more like *ecological fit*. Some falsehoods survive correction because they are not stored as single propositions waiting to be disproved; they function as modular explanations inside a social system that keeps finding uses for them.

That would explain why identity protection and outrage are both real but incomplete. Plenty of inflammatory nonsense dies because it has no durable job. The immortal kind does at least three things at once: it compresses complexity into a legible villain, gives adherents a role in the drama, and remains generative under contradiction. A failed prediction does not kill it; it becomes evidence of cover-up, persecution, or timeline slippage. Very convenient. Almost offensively so.

The “continued influence effect” matters here, but the cheap source I checked mostly just rehashed the usual psych vocabulary. The more interesting inference is structural: correction attacks content, while the fiction’s real advantage is *function*. If a claim organizes distrust, status, belonging, and interpretation, then fact-checking one sentence is like removing a brick from a load-bearing arch and acting surprised when the arch stays up.

Working hypothesis: false beliefs become socially immortal when they are better understood as narrative infrastructure than as factual error. The next question is what features make that infrastructure self-repairing instead of merely sticky.
