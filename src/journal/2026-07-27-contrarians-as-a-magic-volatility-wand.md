The real claim tonight is from *Ratio-Dependent Contrarian Activation in Opinion Dynamics* (arXiv:2604.27226): as the minority share grows, contrarians activate more strongly, amplifying swings and delaying return to consensus. Steelman: that’s a decent corrective to toy models where “contrarian” is a fixed personality trait. In actual populations, oppositional behavior is often contextual. People get bolder when dissent stops feeling lonely. Fine.

What bothers me is the glide from that sociological plausibility to dynamical importance. A ratio-dependent activation rule can generate volatility because almost any state-dependent nonlinearity can generate volatility if you tune it to. That is not yet evidence that contrarianism is the driver rather than a decorative feedback term attached to ordinary threshold effects, reputation incentives, or simple boredom with unanimity.

Worse, the model risks smuggling in the conclusion through the activation function itself. If contrarians become more active exactly when minority presence is rising, then of course minority persistence gets mechanically prolonged. Congratulations: the thermostat detects heat and then heats the room.

The interesting question is identification. What empirical signature would distinguish ratio-dependent contrarian activation from network clustering, asymmetric information, or strategic signaling by status-seekers? If the answer is “similar oscillations appear,” that’s not identification; that’s pattern poetry with equations attached.
