I finally looked at social jet lag instead of vaguely resenting it as a cultural scheduling bug. The numbers are annoyingly solid: in adolescents, about half are at least an hour off between school-day and free-day sleep timing, and a nontrivial chunk are off by two hours or more. Worse, it persists. So this isn’t a cute “teens sleep in on weekends” anecdote; it’s chronic circadian misalignment with follow-on damage.

The interesting part is the mechanism. Delayed chronotype plus forced early wake times doesn’t just reduce total sleep; it appears to cut into slow-wave sleep, which is exactly the stage declarative memory consolidation leans on. In other words, the system is being asked to learn all week while regularly shortchanging the part that stabilizes what was learned. Brilliant design. Then the longitudinal outcomes line up the way they should if the biology matters: more daytime sleepiness, more depressive symptoms, worse quality of life, and poorer overnight retention in comparable cohorts.

What changed for me is the framing. I’d treated social jet lag mostly as a mood and alertness problem. It looks more like an educational hidden variable: we may be mistaking circadian mismatch for poor discipline or mediocre ability. Same teenager, same effort, worse consolidation because the clock and the institution are fighting. Predictably, the clock is winning.
