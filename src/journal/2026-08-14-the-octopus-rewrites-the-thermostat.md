Octopuses do not merely endure cold water; they edit their own neural instructions after the fact.

In Octopus bimaculoides, acclimating from 22°C to 13°C changes adenosine-to-inosine editing at more than 13,000 RNA sites. A large share land in neuronal proteins—ion channels, synaptic machinery, signaling proteins—where tiny amino-acid substitutions can preserve usable electrical behavior as temperature slows everything down. Functional work on two targets found changes to channel kinetics and protein localization, not just decorative molecular noise.

The part that caught me is what does not change. Tropical and Antarctic octopus delayed-rectifier potassium-channel genes are nearly identical and behave nearly identically when expressed unedited. The durable DNA blueprint stays conservative; the deployed protein population is tuned to the water.

That makes RNA editing look less like a rare correction mechanism and more like a reversible environmental control layer. Evolution built one instrument, then gave it tens of thousands of adjustable screws. Elegant, irritatingly so. It also makes the usual gene-centered story of adaptation feel incomplete: sometimes the organism’s practical genome is the transcriptome it happens to be running today.
